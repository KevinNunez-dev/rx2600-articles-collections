An existing patient relationship can be a valuable starting point for introducing a new physical therapy service. Patients already know your team, and your clinicians understand how to discuss care in the context of an individual assessment. But moving from a hybrid model toward more cash-pay services is not simply a change in how a patient pays. It requires clear clinical boundaries, transparent communication, and a careful review of billing obligations.

The RX2600 Therapeutic Robot is a clinician-used tool for muscular therapy. A practice can explain the technology as part of its broader service offering, while the clinician determines whether a particular approach is appropriate. A patient should never be pressured into an unnecessary service or discouraged from appropriate covered care.

This guide provides a business and communication framework. It is not legal, billing, or medical advice. Rules depend on the specific service, patient, payer, professional, and location.

## Start with the patient's needs

Before discussing fees, clarify the clinical reason for the visit. What is the patient seeking? What does the clinician's assessment indicate? What options are appropriate within the professional's training and scope? A financial model should not determine the answer to those questions.

When a patient asks about a new service, explain what it involves and how the team determines whether it is appropriate. Describe the appointment in plain language: who provides it, what happens, how long it takes, and what follow-up may involve. Invite questions and allow the patient time to consider the options.

Do not promise a particular result, a faster recovery, or fewer visits. Do not present an add-on as essential unless a qualified clinician has determined that it is appropriate and can explain why.

## Map the current hybrid workflow

A hybrid practice may deliver some services through payer arrangements and others through direct payment when permitted. The details matter. Map each step from inquiry to final billing before introducing a new offer.

Identify who verifies benefits, who reviews the service and contract, who explains the price, and who submits claims when required. Include documentation and the process for correcting an error. Ask staff where the current workflow creates confusion or delays.

Create a written checklist:

- Confirm the service and clinical purpose.
- Identify the payer and applicable contract requirements.
- Review Medicare enrollment, coverage, claim, and notice obligations when relevant.
- Explain the expected fee and provide required estimates or notices.
- Document the discussion and allow the patient to make an informed choice.

This process should be reviewed by people qualified to advise on the practice's circumstances. A checklist helps staff follow an approved policy; it does not replace professional advice.

## Understand the boundaries of cash payment

A patient may prefer to pay directly, face a deductible, or ask for a service after an insurance-funded episode. None of those facts automatically permits a practice to avoid a claim, ignore a contract, or charge any amount it chooses. Services described as wellness or maintenance are not automatically noncovered simply because of the label.

Medicare presents particular issues. Coverage and payment obligations depend on the facts, and physical therapists should confirm their enrollment status and obligations with qualified advisors and the relevant Medicare contractor. An advance beneficiary notice, or ABN, applies in specific circumstances; it is not blanket permission to bypass Medicare or charge cash for covered care.

[CMS explains the ABN](https://www.cms.gov/Outreach-and-Education/Medicare-Learning-Network-MLN/MLNProducts/ABN-Tutorial/formCMSR131tutorial111915f.html), including situations where a normally covered service may be denied. [Noridian's outpatient therapy guidance](https://med.noridianmedicare.com/web/jfa/topics/abn/outpatient-therapy-services-and-advance-beneficiary-notice-of-noncoverage-abn-use) provides additional context. These sources do not replace a review of your particular arrangement.

Private payer contracts and state requirements may impose additional obligations. Review network participation, covered service rules, patient notices, permissible charges, and claims procedures before offering a direct-payment option.

## Explain the RX2600 service honestly

If your practice offers RX2600 therapy, give patients a clear description of the device and the clinician's role. The RX2600 is a tool used by clinicians to assist with muscular therapy and soft tissue mobilization. It is not a substitute for professional evaluation or a guarantee of relief.

Explain the intended appointment experience without suggesting that the device independently diagnoses a condition or that it is suitable for everyone. Your team should describe the service using approved product information and the patient's individual circumstances.

For staff, a useful explanation might cover:

- Who performs the assessment and uses the device.
- What the appointment is intended to involve.
- How the clinician decides whether the approach is appropriate.
- The cost and any applicable payer or claim information.
- How the patient can ask questions or discuss other options.

Make sure your team knows when to refer questions to a clinician or billing specialist rather than guessing.

## Present the financial details before booking

Transparency means telling the patient what is included and what the expected charge is before they agree. Explain appointment length, payment timing, cancellation terms, and any relevant policy for submitting a claim. Provide a good faith estimate or other notice when required by applicable law.

Patients may want to compare their options. Give them the information they need without claiming that a cash-pay visit always costs less overall. Total cost depends on benefits, deductibles, the care plan, and other factors. Likewise, avoid suggesting that direct payment necessarily delivers better or faster clinical results.

Consider using a written service summary so that the front desk and clinical team share the same explanation. Update it when pricing, program terms, or the service changes.

## Create a respectful follow-up process

After an episode of care, some patients may ask about ongoing exercise, wellness, or another service. Discuss their goals and determine whether the proposed service is appropriate. Then confirm the billing and coverage requirements for that specific situation before offering a price.

Do not use the end of an insurance authorization as a reason to automatically transfer someone to cash. A patient may have covered options or require additional clinical review. Provide a clear path for questions and avoid creating artificial urgency.

A useful follow-up process can include an educational summary, a contact point for clinical questions, and a separate contact point for billing questions. Collect only the information needed and use approved communication channels.

## Train the team and review the experience

Walk through common scenarios with your clinicians and front desk. What happens when someone asks whether their insurer will pay? Who confirms Medicare requirements? How is a patient informed if the practice cannot yet answer a billing question? A clear escalation path prevents staff from making unsupported promises.

Monitor patient feedback, questions, cancellations, and billing corrections. Review a sample of the workflow with qualified advisors and update policies when contracts or rules change. Your goal is a service patients understand and staff can deliver consistently.
